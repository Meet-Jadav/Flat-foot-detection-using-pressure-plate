Module 1 — The Problem and The Data
1.1 What is Flatfoot Clinically
The human foot has a medial longitudinal arch — the curved gap you see on the inner side of a normal foot when it stands. This arch acts like a spring. It absorbs shock, distributes weight, and enables efficient walking.
In flatfoot (medically called Pes Planus), this arch is reduced or completely collapsed. The entire inner side of the foot touches the ground.
There are two types you should know for your project:
Flexible Flatfoot — arch disappears when standing but reappears when sitting or on tiptoe. Very common, especially in children. This is what your pressure plate will mostly see.
Rigid Flatfoot — arch is gone permanently regardless of position. Less common, more serious.
Your device captures the standing (static) case — so you are primarily dealing with flexible flatfoot detection.

1.2 Why Pressure Plate is the Right Tool
There are multiple ways doctors assess flatfoot:

The pressure plate wins because it gives you numbers — and numbers can be fed into algorithms. This is exactly why this hardware exists in clinical and research settings.

1.3 What Your OHM 3000 Actually Captures
Your device has roughly a 54×54 grid of sensors across a 38×38 cm plate.
When someone stands on it, each sensor measures how much force is pressing down on it. The result is a matrix like this — imagine just a 6×6 small version for simplicity:
 0    0    0    0    0    0
  0   15   40   35    0    0
  0    8   55   60   20    0
  0    0    5   10    0    0   ← this row is the arch region
  0   20   45   50   25    0
  0   10   30   40   15    0

Each number is pressure in some unit (kPa or raw sensor units). Zero means no contact. Higher number means more force at that point.
Now for a flatfoot, that row marked as arch region would NOT be zeros — it would have values like 30, 40, 25 because the collapsed arch is touching the plate.
This is the entire basis of your project. Everything else — formulas, ML models, CNNs — is just a more and more sophisticated way of detecting this one thing.

1.4 The Three Zones — Foundation of Every Formula
Every paper you will read divides the foot into zones. The most standard division is three horizontal zones:
|  ZONE 3 — Forefoot + Toes  |  (top third)
|-----------------------------|
|  ZONE 2 — Midfoot / Arch   |  (middle third)  ← KEY ZONE
|-----------------------------|
|  ZONE 1 — Heel             |  (bottom third)

In your 54 row grid:
Zone 1 = rows 36 to 54 (heel)
Zone 2 = rows 18 to 36 (arch)
Zone 3 = rows 0 to 18 (forefoot)
Arch Index formula directly uses these zones:
AI = Contact Area in Zone 2 / Total Contact Area

Contact area means — count of cells where pressure is above a minimum threshold (say, above 5 units to filter noise).
Normal foot: Zone 2 has very little contact → AI is small (below 0.21) Flatfoot: Zone 2 has lots of contact → AI is large (above 0.26)

1.5 What Research Papers Say About This Formula
Papers from clinical biomechanics (Cavanagh & Rodgers, 1987 — the original Arch Index paper) established these thresholds and they have been used for 30+ years. Almost every ML paper in this domain uses Arch Index as either:
The label generator — they compute AI to decide if a foot is flat or normal, then use that as ground truth for training
The baseline — they compare their model accuracy against pure AI thresholding
This means even if you build a CNN, you will still need to understand and implement Arch Index — because that is how you will label your data.

1.6 Beyond Arch Index — Other Features Papers Extract
Research papers that use classical ML don't just use AI. They extract multiple features from the pressure grid:
Pressure based features:
Maximum pressure value in each zone
Average pressure in each zone
Total contact area
Forefoot to hindfoot pressure ratio
Geometric features:
Foot length and width from the grid
Midfoot width at narrowest point (used in Chippaux Smirak Index)
Center of Pressure (CoP):
This is a weighted average of where all the pressure is concentrated:
CoP_x = sum(pressure[i][j] × j) / sum(pressure[i][j])
CoP_y = sum(pressure[i][j] × i) / sum(pressure[i][j])

In a flatfoot, CoP shifts medially (inward) compared to normal. Papers use this as a feature for ML models.
Why this matters: When you move to classical ML in Module 3, these are exactly the features you will compute and feed into your model. The quality of these features directly determines model performance — this is called feature engineering and it is a skill in itself.

Module 2 — Rule Based Approach
2.1 The Core Idea
Rule based approach means — no learning, no training, no model. Just math on the pressure grid.
You compute a number from the grid. You compare it to a threshold. You make a decision.
Simple. Transparent. Clinically validated for 30+ years.

2.2 Arch Index — Deep Dive
You already know the formula. Now understand every word of it.
AI = Contact Area in Zone 2 / Total Contact Area

What is "contact area"?
Not the pressure value — the count of sensors that are actually touching the foot.
A sensor is "in contact" if its pressure value is above some minimum threshold. Why threshold? Because even when no foot is there, sensors pick up tiny noise values. So you say — if pressure is above 5 units, that sensor is in contact.
So contact area is simply:
count of cells where pressure > threshold

What is Zone 2?
The middle horizontal third of the foot bounding box. Not the middle third of the entire grid — the middle third of where the foot actually is. This distinction matters when feet of different sizes step on the same plate.

2.3 The Bounding Box Problem
This is something most beginners miss and most papers address.
Your grid is 54×54. A foot does not fill the entire grid. It occupies maybe 20×40 cells somewhere in the middle. Before you divide into zones, you need to find where the foot is in the grid.
This is called finding the bounding box:
top row    = first row that has any contact
bottom row = last row that has any contact
left col   = first column that has any contact
right col  = last column that has any contact

Then you divide THIS region into three equal zones — not the full grid.
Without this step, your Arch Index will be wrong because Zone 2 might not align with the actual midfoot.

2.4 Step by Step — How You Will Compute AI
Here is the logic in plain English — you will convert this to code:
1. Load the CSV grid
2. Apply threshold → create a binary map (1 = contact, 0 = no contact)
3. Find bounding box of the foot
4. Divide bounding box into 3 equal horizontal zones
5. Count contact cells in Zone 2 → this is middle_area
6. Count total contact cells → this is total_area
7. AI = middle_area / total_area
8. If AI > 0.26 → Flatfoot
   If AI < 0.21 → Normal or High Arch
   If between → Borderline

That is the complete rule based system. Eight steps.

2.5 Chippaux Smirak Index — The Second Formula
This one works differently. Instead of area, it uses width.
CSI = (minimum width of midfoot / maximum width of forefoot) × 100

Visually:
       ████████████████          ← forefoot, measure MAX width here
        ██████████████
        ████████                  ← midfoot, measure MIN width here
        ██████
        ████████████████          ← heel

For a flatfoot, the midfoot is almost as wide as the forefoot — so CSI is high. For a normal foot, the midfoot narrows significantly — CSI is low.
How to compute width from your grid: For each row in the foot region, count how many consecutive contact cells exist. That count is the width at that row.
Minimum width across midfoot rows = narrowest point of arch. Maximum width across forefoot rows = widest point of ball of foot.

2.6 What Papers Found About Rule Based Approach
Several early papers (pre-2015) relied purely on Arch Index or CSI for flatfoot detection. Here is the honest picture:
Where it works well:
Clear cut cases — severely flat or clearly normal
Controlled clinical settings with consistent measurement protocol
Quick screening of large populations
Where it fails:
Problem 1 — Threshold sensitivity The 0.21 and 0.26 thresholds were derived from a specific population (mostly Western adults). Studies on Asian, pediatric, or elderly populations found different natural distributions. Your Indian subject population may need recalibrated thresholds.
Problem 2 — Foot size variation A child's foot and an adult's foot give different absolute areas. The ratio partially handles this but not perfectly.
Problem 3 — Zone division is rigid Dividing into exact thirds is anatomically approximate. The actual arch region does not start and end at exactly one third and two thirds of foot length.
Problem 4 — No severity grading AI tells you flat or not flat. It cannot tell you how flat. For clinical use, severity matters — mild, moderate, severe flatfoot need different interventions.
Problem 5 — Single static snapshot Rule based methods use one standing measurement. Dynamic walking data contains much more information about arch function that formulas cannot capture.

2.7 Why You Still Need This
Despite all those problems — you MUST implement this first. Three reasons:
Reason 1 — It generates your labels When you collect data from real subjects, who decides which foot is flatfoot and which is normal? You cannot ask a doctor for every sample. Arch Index gives you an automated labeling system for your dataset. Every ML paper does this.
Reason 2 — It is your baseline Whatever model you build later, your first question will be — is my model better than just using the formula? If your CNN gets 85% but Arch Index alone gets 83%, your CNN barely added value. You need this number.
Reason 3 — It is explainable Your sir asks why your model classified a foot as flat. With rule based, you can say — Zone 2 contact area was 847 cells out of 2100 total, giving AI of 0.40 which is above threshold. With a CNN you cannot explain it that cleanly.
